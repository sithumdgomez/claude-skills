# Brand Developer

A workflow for taking a client from brand discovery to a finished, production-ready identity system. This mirrors how a senior branding developer/designer actually works — strategy first, then design, then the technical translation into usable assets — not just "make a logo."

## For other skills that want to use this one

There are two valid ways another skill can lean on this one — pick whichever fits:

1. **Invoke it as a sub-skill** (via the Skill tool, name `brand-developer`) when you want the full workflow applied to a client engagement, or a brand-consistency/critique check run against existing assets.
2. **Read this file directly** when you just need the process/checkpoints as a reference without running the full flow.

## Why this order matters

Skipping straight to visuals without locking positioning first is the most common way branding projects go sideways — clients end up with a nice-looking logo that doesn't hold up once it's used across five different contexts. Each phase below exists to catch a specific kind of problem before it becomes expensive to fix.

## Workflow

### 1. Discovery

Five inputs feed this phase, and all should be gathered before any visual work starts:

**Niche research** — study the client's industry and competitor landscape: what visual conventions dominate the space, what everyone's competitors look like (so the brand can deliberately stand apart or deliberately fit in, as a choice rather than an accident), and what the audience actually responds to in this category.

**Real touchpoint audit** — before forming opinions, gather how the client actually presents today: current site, social profiles, physical/packaging presence, competitor visuals side-by-side. This mirrors Saul Bass's method on the Continental Airlines identity — a documented 1,500-photo audit of the client's real-world touchpoints before any concepting began. Research plus a real audit is what makes Discovery evidence-based rather than opinion-based.

**Branding questionnaire data** — go through the client's completed questionnaire responses in full before forming opinions. Pull out:
- **Positioning**: what they do that competitors don't, and who it's for
- **Tone**: the adjectives *they* used to describe how they want to be perceived (don't substitute your own without checking against theirs)
- **Constraints**: existing brand equity to preserve (if rebrand), tier/budget, timeline, required deliverables (digital only? print? signage?)

If a questionnaire hasn't been completed yet, ask for it (or the raw answers) before generating concepts — research plus a completed questionnaire is what separates a positioned brand from a guess.

**Past-work calibration** — if the agency has prior work in a similar niche or of comparable scope, review 2-3 comparable past projects before concepting. This keeps new work consistent with the agency's established voice and quality bar, and surfaces patterns worth repeating or deliberately breaking from. If no comparable past work exists, skip this without asking — it's a calibration aid, not a hard requirement.

**Decision-maker access** — identify the actual final decision-maker before any design work starts, and get them into the room for at least the kickoff and the eventual concept presentation. Presenting to an intermediary throughout and meeting the real decision-maker only at concept reveal creates a stranger-pitching-a-stranger dynamic (per Sagi Haviv of Chermayeff & Geismar & Haviv) and removes the rapport needed to advocate for a direction if they react badly.

**Lock before moving on** — before Mood Boards begin, turn Discovery into two written artifacts the client explicitly confirms: a one-paragraph positioning statement (so visual work starts from an agreed strategy, not an assumption), and a scope of work (deliverable list + revision-round limit). A verbal understanding of scope is not the same as a written one — skipping this is the single most common cause of a branding project's timeline and budget quietly unraveling.

### 2. Mood Boards

Before concepting, translate the research and questionnaire findings into 1-2 mood boards to visualize direction and pressure-test the "feel" with the client before committing design hours to full concepts. Each board should pull from:
- Color and material references that match the tone words from the questionnaire
- Competitor/niche visual references (to show what's being intentionally echoed or avoided)
- Type and imagery style references

Get client sign-off (or at least a clear reaction) on the mood board direction before moving into concept development — it's much cheaper to redirect at the mood board stage than after full logo concepts are built.

**De-risking checkpoint** — before moving to full concept fidelity, run one cheap, fast proof pass (a quick mockup, a single rough application in a real context) to test whether the chosen direction actually holds up. This mirrors Saul Bass's method on Hitchcock's *Psycho*: he shot cheap test footage against his storyboards eight days before the real shoot to prove the sequence worked before committing to full production. A cheap proof now is cheaper than a rejected concept later.

### 3. Concept Development

AI models — and tired designers — default to the most *probable* output, which is the same as the most *generic* one. Research on "mode collapse" confirms this pull toward the average is structural: critically, telling yourself (or a model) to "be creative" / "be original" does **not** escape it, and neither does role-playing a famous designer ("be Paul Rand") — genius-persona framing actually narrows ideas. (Applying a designer's documented *method* as technique, the way this skill cites Bass/Rand/Haviv, is different from wearing them as a persona.) Creativity here comes from **structural constraint and forced combination**, applied deliberately. Run the method below before committing to directions.

Two layers run throughout: the **thinking layer** (strategy, blends, critique — done in words, in conversation) and the **canvas layer** (rendering and physically testing the actual marks — done by the designer in their tools). This skill drives the thinking layer and hands the canvas layer a clear brief; it never pretends to render or blur an actual image itself.

**Step 1 — Name the friction.** Before any visual idea, state the core *contradiction* in the business — the tension a generic logo would paper over (e.g. "institutional-grade security that has to feel warm and accessible to everyday parents"). The most memorable identities resolve a real tension; naming it first is what stops the work sliding to the category default.

**Step 2 — Ban the clichés, explicitly.** List the category's clichés out loud and forbid them for the concepting pass — and ban *two* kinds, because missing the second is how generic work sneaks back in disguised as "divergent":
- **Iconographic clichés** — the stock symbols: no leaf/green/globe for eco, no circuit-board/node for tech, no bean/cup/steam-swirl for coffee.
- **Stylistic / treatment clichés** — the category's *fashionable house style* of the moment, which is a cliché even when the imagery is original: e.g. risograph + ink-stamp + hand-lettered signage for third-wave coffee, or minimalist-sans-on-white for wellness. A trendy execution everyone in the category is already using is not a distinctive direction, no matter how nice it looks.

This isn't attitude: explicit constraint injection is one of the most evidence-backed ways to force originality, because it blocks the memorized obvious answer from being regurgitated.

**Step 3 — Blend to generate.** Use conceptual blending (Fauconnier & Turner's framework: two unrelated "input spaces" fused into a third with *emergent* meaning neither had alone — the way "houseboat" is more than house + boat). Force the brand's essence together with an unrelated domain — an object, an action, a subculture, a mechanism — and look for the emergent form: *"this accounting firm as if it were a lighthouse keeper / a jazz standard / a Swiss watch movement."* Generate the connection, then push it until a coherent, ownable idea appears. Forced cross-domain analogy is empirically what pulls ideation out of the "familiar semantic neighbourhood." **Watch for false blends:** a domain that's just an association of the brand's *name* or category (e.g. reaching for cartography because the client is called "Ninth Parallel," or coffee-cherries because they roast coffee) is a semantic neighbour, not a genuine collision — it feels clever but stays inside the default. Force a domain the brand has *no* obvious link to, and make the relevance emerge from the blend.

**Step 4 — Defamiliarize to check.** Take each promising blend and estrange it (Shklovsky's *ostranenie* — "making the familiar strange" so the viewer actually *sees* it instead of skimming past). Does it show the category from an unfamiliar angle, scale, material, or context — or withhold the obvious signifier entirely? If a first-time viewer wouldn't do a small double-take, it's probably still riding the default. Blending invents; defamiliarization keeps the invention from reading as expected.

**Step 5 — Generate wide, converge narrow.** Don't jump straight to 2-3 directions — that's where the average creeps back in. Internally generate 5-10 genuinely divergent candidates first (the "generate many, then select" pattern measurably raises diversity over one-shotting), each with a one-line strategic rationale, then converge to the **2-3 most distinct and most defensible** to actually develop and present. Think wide; present narrow.

Each of the 2-3 directions you converge on should still differ in underlying logic (one built around a wordmark + custom letterform, one around a symbol/icon system, one around a type-driven approach) so the client is choosing a strategy, not a color. For each, define the full spec — don't skip parts of it, since a half-specced direction reads as a weaker option than it is:
- Logo lockup — **both** the primary lockup *and* the secondary/icon-only variant, not just the primary
- Color palette (primary + secondary), with hex **and CMYK** values — CMYK is load-bearing, not optional, whenever the brand's medium includes print/packaging (bags, cards, signage); skipping it there is a real gap
- Typography — name **actual typefaces** for the heading + body pairing, not just a category ("a precise grotesque"); a named pairing is the difference between a real direction and a sketch of one
- One example application (business card, social post, or packaging mockup) — present it in person or over synchronous video, never async or as a single flat image, and show it across at least two real contexts so the client reacts to how the mark lives in the world, not a logo floating on white. Open by setting the expectation that first reactions are unreliable — a strong mark is rarely love at first sight (per Sagi Haviv's own practice at Chermayeff & Geismar & Haviv, where a client once rejected a mark shown as a single printout, then approved the same design once shown across its real applications)

**Rationale & Critique** — judge each direction against three criteria, not vibes: **Appropriateness** (does it fit the client's actual personality, from the questionnaire and audit, not a generic industry look), **Memorability** (is it distinctive enough to stick, or does it blend into the competitor audit from Discovery), **Simplicity** (does it hold up and flex across every application it'll actually be used in). A direction that fails one of these needs a reason, not just a redesign. Test simplicity specifically against Paul Rand's framing: is this simple *because* the idea is resolved, or simple because we stopped trying?

**A note on how many directions to show** — 2-3 distinct directions is the default here, and works well when the client needs to choose a strategy collaboratively. But know the alternative: Paul Rand's own practice was to present exactly one considered solution, arguing a batch of options signals designer uncertainty rather than client choice. If your conviction on the brief and the client relationship are strong enough, presenting one is a legitimate, deliberate call — not a shortcut. Decide per engagement; don't default to more options as a hedge.

**Trademark clearance, before the client gets attached** — once directions are narrowed to a shortlist (not after one is fully refined), run a preliminary trademark search on any proposed name/wordmark component. Do this before presenting the shortlist, not after — a client who has emotionally committed to a mark that turns out to be unavailable is a much harder conversation than swapping one shortlist option for another. Sagi Haviv's firm, Chermayeff & Geismar & Haviv, adopted this exact practice specifically to keep every remaining shortlist option viable; treat it as a design-process step, not a legal afterthought.

### 4. Refinement
Once a direction is chosen, this is where most of the actual craft time goes:
- **Reduction / recognizability test** — a strong mark survives being blurred or shrunk (Rand's principle, judged against his six criteria for an effective logo: distinctiveness, visibility, adaptability, memorability, universality, timelessness). *Thinking layer:* reason about whether the concept leans on fine detail or gradients to work. *Canvas layer:* the designer renders and actually checks it — 16×16 favicon, single-color, reversed, and (Rand's own bar) still readable stamped in rough black ink on a cardboard box. If it only holds up at full size and full color, simplify the concept, don't just shrink it.
- Confirm color accessibility (contrast ratios) if the identity will be used in UI
- Build out the full type scale, not just the two hero fonts
- Check the system holds together across at least 3 different deliverable types (e.g. business card + Instagram post + signage)
- If the identity is digital-first, confirm a dark-mode-safe variant and any motion/animation rules now — these are core deliverables for many 2026 digital brands, not afterthoughts. Skip this outright for print/physical-first clients where it doesn't apply.

### 5. Brand Guideline Documentation
Every finished identity needs a guideline doc so the brand stays consistent once it leaves your hands. At minimum, include:
- Logo usage rules (clear space, minimum size, incorrect usage examples)
- Full color palette with exact values
- Typography hierarchy
- Voice/tone notes if copywriting is in scope
- Application examples

Use the `document-skills:docx` skill if this needs to be delivered as a polished Word document, or `document-skills:pptx` if it's being presented as a deck.

### 6. Technical Translation / Build
If the deliverable includes a working build (website, template system, packaging file):
- Confirm the target platform/format before starting, then hand off to the skill that owns it: `cms-website-builder` for WordPress/Wix/Shopify, `build-premium-website` for a React marketing site, `web-app-development` for an app/dashboard with real backend logic, or `frontend-design` for a hand-coded component/page. This skill sets the brand direction; those execute the build.
- Build with reusable components/tokens (color variables, type styles) rather than one-off hardcoded values, so future pages stay on-brand without redesigning each one
- Run a design QA pass against the guideline doc before handoff — check every deliverable actually matches the documented rules, not just "looks right"

### 7. Final Delivery Package

Once the design is finalized and approved, export and hand off the complete file package — this is the actual product the client is paying for, so nothing gets delivered "when I get a chance." Package typically includes:

- **Brand source files** — editable originals so the client (or a future designer) can modify the work later. Typical formats for this toolset: Adobe Illustrator (`.ai`), Adobe Photoshop (`.psd`), Affinity Designer (`.afdesign`), Affinity Studio (`.af`), and Figma (`.fig`) where relevant
- **Logo exports** — the full format set: vector (.svg, .eps) and raster (.png with transparency, .jpg) versions, each in primary, secondary/icon-only, single-color, and reversed/white variants
- **Mockups** — the application examples produced during refinement, exported as final images
- **Guideline document** — the finished brand guide from step 5
- **Supporting images** — any brand photography, textures, or graphics used across the identity

Before sending, do a completeness check against this list rather than assuming everything got exported along the way — it's easy to finish the design and forget a format variant that was only used once during refinement.

## Output expectations

- Don't present a single logo option as "the design" — always frame concepts as strategic directions with tradeoffs, even if only briefly
- Match fidelity to project stage: rough/conceptual in early rounds, pixel-precise only once a direction is locked
- When producing files (guideline doc, presentation, mockups), check the relevant skill (`document-skills:docx`/`document-skills:pptx`/`document-skills:pdf`) for the actual file creation — this skill governs the *brand thinking*, not file mechanics

## Common pitfalls to catch

- A logo that only works on white/light backgrounds (always test on dark)
- A palette with no defined secondary/accent colors, forcing improvisation later
- A guideline doc with rules but no "incorrect usage" examples — clients need to see what NOT to do
- Treating the brief as fixed when the client's positioning is vague — better to ask a clarifying question than build on a shaky foundation
- A verbal-only scope agreement — a project without a written SOW is the most common way a good design gets buried under uncontrolled revisions
